# Little Ages

A pixel-art civilization sim where nothing is scripted. Every person has their own needs, personality, family, memories and knowledge, and acts for themselves. Tribes, leaders, kingdoms, revolts, trade, religions, wars and inventions only happen because people decided they were worth doing. Worlds run from the Stone Age toward the Space Age on huge procedural maps with a real underground.

- **Plan:** [`docs/PLAN.md`](docs/PLAN.md)
- **Style board:** [`style-board/`](style-board/), a live pixel-art board used to choose the game's look. It's also published as an artifact for picking styles.

## Status

Milestone 1, "A living world", is finished, in three checkpoints:

- **A. The world** (done). Plate-tectonic continents, mountain ranges, wind-driven rain and rain shadows, rivers and lakes, Whittaker biomes, 21 tree species that change with the seasons, and living wildlife. Herds, packs, bears, birds, rabbits and fish breed, starve, migrate and hunt, all balanced by what the land can feed.
- **B. The people** (done). Every person is a real individual:
  - **Genes.** A diploid genome of real loci for skin, hair color and curl, red hair and blue eyes (recessive), height, build, beards, baldness, graying and temperament. Founders get ancestries that fit the sun and cold where they live. Children inherit one copy of each gene from each parent, with the odd mutation, so families look alike and mixed couples have children in between.
  - **Minds.** Needs (food, water, rest, warmth, company, safety, health), mood, eight heritable temperament traits, skills, knowledge (fire, snares, fishing, hides, spears), memories, and opinions of everyone they know. Each person keeps their own mental map of places (berry patches, fishing spots, game, water, other camps) and learns more by exploring and by talk.
  - **A utility AI with a "why?".** Everyone weighs what matters most right now, makes a step-by-step plan, and can always tell you why in plain words ("Gathering berries at the patch west of camp; the band only has food for two days and she has three children to feed").
  - **Bands.** Shared food stores, the fire, households and shelters in each culture's style, sharing norms, a respected elder. Bands move camp when the land runs out, split when they grow too big, take in families from kin bands, and meet strangers. Peoples have their own languages, names, clothing, dyes, hairstyles and marriage customs.
  - **The life cycle.** Courtship and couples (never close kin), pregnancy and nursing, babies carried, toddlers, children who play and learn skills by watching their parents, teens, adults, elders, illness, injuries, hunting accidents, animal attacks, hunger, grief and fostering orphans.
  - **On screen.** Hand-drawn pixel people with their genes' colors and their culture's clothes, 24 animations, thought bubbles, camps with fires and shelters, band labels, a pinned tag for anyone you click (with their why, needs, family, friends, rivals and memories) and a news feed.
- **C. The interface** (done). Everything that has happened can be found, replayed and followed:
  - **Every event is weighed.** The simulation gives each event a weight from 0 to 1 and a headline, from what it was (a band ending outweighs a birth), who it happened to (the death of the one a band listens to counts for more) and how rare it is in this world so far (the first meeting of two peoples, the first mammoth). Nothing is scripted; the weight only describes what people did.
  - **The history ribbon** along the bottom. Seasons run along its foot, the head count rises and falls above them, and every notable moment stands as a banner, a little speech bubble of what it was about. Heavier events are bigger, and zooming in shows the smaller ones between. Hover a banner to read it, click it to fly there and pin the people in it, or drag along the ribbon to scrub through the past: the camera hops to where each thing happened, and letting go settles on the last one. The wheel zooms, shift-drag slides, YEAR / 5Y / ALL set how much shows, the arrows step between notable moments, and NOW brings you back to the present.
  - **The journal.** Every event, newest first, under the year and season. Filter by kind, by a few words, or to the notable. Open it on one person's or one band's own story from their tag, or on someone who has died from search.
  - **Search.** Press `/` (or `Ctrl+K`) and type any part of a name: the living, the dead (found where they died, with how), bands, the camps they sit in, whole peoples, and moments in history. With nothing typed it offers the people each band listens to.
  - **Story Spotlight.** Switch it on (`L`) and the camera goes looking for the story: it flies to the most worthwhile thing that has happened lately, pins and follows the people in it, slows the clock a little for the biggest moments, and when nothing is happening follows an ordinary person through their day. Pan, zoom or click and it waits for you.
  - **Bonds.** Switch on BONDS (`B`) and every couple, family tie, friendship and rivalry in view is a thread on the map: rose, green, blue and dashed red. They follow people as they walk. Kin of a pinned person who are out of view get an arrow at the screen's edge saying who and how far; press it to go there.

Milestone 2, "First villages", is built in three checkpoints:

- **A. Sharing, friends and feuds, and the first leaders** (done). Bands are no longer one shared larder, and nobody is made leader:
  - **Hearths and the pot.** Food brought back to camp is split between the band's pot, which anyone may eat from, and the household's own hearth. How much goes in the pot is up to whoever brought it, within their people's custom: meat from a big kill is nearly always shared (more so in the heat, when it would spoil), plant food mostly feeds the gatherer's family, the generous share more and the greedy less. People eat from their own hearth first, then the pot, then from close family's hearths without needing to ask.
  - **Asking and the evening round.** When the hearth and the pot are bare and the children are hungry, people walk over and ask a household that has food: kin first, then friends and those who owe them. Most people give, and hardly anyone turns away someone starving. Twice a day food also passes between hearths unasked, close kin first, evening things out so that a lean week means everyone going a little hungry rather than a few starving. Food given is remembered as a debt and paid back from the next good haul. A full hearth that turns a hungry family away is remembered too, and the whole camp notices.
  - **Feuds.** Wrongs add up to a grudge that fades over weeks: being turned away hungry, losing a sweetheart to someone else, a partner leaving for another, being hurt on someone's hunt, a freeloader who never brings anything in, quarrels that keep coming back, a leader pushed aside. Enough of it, in someone stubborn and hot-tempered, hardens into a feud. Both families take sides and come to dislike each other, feuding people will not share or hunt together, and face to face by the fire they can come to blows. A respected go-between can talk each side round. A feud can drive a family out to another band, split a band in two, or pass to a grown child when one side dies, and children growing up take on their parents' grudges.
  - **Leaders.** Every adult goes along with somebody's word: a seasoned hunter, a wise elder, their own father, or their own judgment. Whoever most of the band listens to leads it, and wears a feather in their hair. Those who listen to them answer their call to hunt, they are expected to share and to settle quarrels, and when a band splits, people go with whoever they listen to. They are replaced only by someone with a clearly bigger following, and when a second voice gathers nearly as many, the band can split along the line. Where people share freely, a proud hunter who carries in a big kill is told it is a bag of bones, so that nobody grows too big.
  - **Friends far away.** People walk over to visit kin and friends in other bands, bringing a gift (beads, ochre, a good blade), and come home with news of where the food is. Gifts are remembered and returned, and in hard times a family goes to live where it has friends.
  - **On screen.** The tag shows each person's hearth and the band's pot, whom they listen to and how many listen to them, how much they share, who owes whom, and every feud they are part of and why. The band card shows its leader and any second voice, every hearth's food, how much went into the pot yesterday, and the band's feuds. BONDS draws feuds as heavy dark red lines and a fine gold line from each adult to the one they listen to, so a leader sits in a star of gold. The journal has a new group, "Friends and feuds", and the ribbon and Spotlight pick up feuds, fights, peace, new leaders, two voices, gifts between peoples, and hunters taken down a peg.
- **B. The first discoveries** (done). Knowledge lives only in heads, and nothing is handed out:
  - **The old ways.** When the story starts, every people knows how to keep a fire alive, hunt with spears, fish, work hides and set snares. Nobody can make fire from nothing, and nobody has blades, axes, digging sticks, baskets, nets, weirs, a way to keep meat, or a single planted seed.
  - **Working things out.** A new way of doing things only comes to someone in the middle of related work, and mostly under pressure. Someone knapping a new point after their spear broke strikes long blades. A hungry gatherer scrabbling for roots in hard ground hardens a stick in the fire. Someone with more food in the patch than they can carry weaves a basket. A poor catch makes someone knot a net, and fish crowding a river's shallows make someone drive in a weir. Meat rotting after a big kill goes up in strips over the smoke. Seeds spilled around a camp lived in long enough come up as shoots. The curious, the skilled and the hard-working see it most often, and each idea builds on others: no nets without fishing and the cord of snares, no axe before blades, no planting before digging sticks.
  - **Fire.** Keeping a fire is old; making one is a discovery. A band that cannot make fire guards its fire, feeds it whatever the weather, and carries embers when it moves. If the fire dies, someone walks to a neighbor's camp and carries an ember home in a bundle of moss, while others twirl sticks and strike stones by the cold hearth, and one of them may get the tinder to catch. Without fire there is no warming up, and meat eaten raw goes less far.
  - **Passing it on.** Children pick up what their parents and the band know as they grow up. Anyone can pick up a trick by watching someone at it close by. In the evenings by the fire people show each other how: kin and friends first, the kind and sociable most, the greedy less to anyone not their own. People who marry into another band or go to live there take what they know with them, and now and then a visit passes something on. How well someone knows a thing grows each time they do it, and a master teaches faster.
  - **Lost with the last who knew it.** When the last person in a band who knew something dies or leaves, the band no longer knows it; when the last anywhere is gone, it is lost to the world until someone works it out again. Things people make stay where they are made, and someone passing an old weir can work out how it was done.
  - **What it changes.** Blades make points that break less, cut more meat from each carcass and scrape hides into clothes that last. Axes bring in more firewood. Digging sticks reach roots and tubers in the lean seasons and on open grassland. Baskets carry half as much again and keep plant food dry. Nets catch far more fish. A weir traps fish day and night until the floods knock it about and someone mends it. Dried meat keeps for months instead of days. A household that sows a patch by camp in spring has a harvest at summer's end, but only if the band is still there, which gives it a reason to stay.
  - **On screen.** The tag lists what someone knows, how well, and how they came to know it ("from her mother, Ama", "worked it out"). The band card lists the band's know-how and who holds it, warns when only one person does or when nobody can make fire, and shows its weirs and plots and the dried meat put by. IDEAS (`I`) is the world's record: every idea worked out so far, who first did it and when (click to fly there), how many know it now in how many bands and peoples, what has been lost, and what nobody has thought of yet. Weirs stand in the rivers, plots go from bare rows to green to gold, and drying racks hang by the shelters. The journal has a new group, "Discoveries", the ribbon and Spotlight pick up firsts, and search finds know-how by name.
- **C. First villages** (this branch). Nobody founds a village: it is what the game calls a place people stopped leaving.
  - **Staying put.** Thin days only push a band on as far as they eat into food that keeps: a band with weeks of grain, roots and dried meat put by is in no hurry, and a band without a real store moves on as it always has (fresh meat rots in days and is no store). What a household would leave behind (food in its pit, its fields' coming harvest, a hut that took days to raise) holds people back too, more so with a winter coming, but only while they are fed: a hut never keeps a hungry band in a place with nothing left. A band living in huts either stays or goes somewhere new; it does not shift its fire a little way and leave its huts standing empty.
  - **Stores.** Storage pits are the first of two new ideas. Someone with more food at the hearth than the family can eat before it spoils, in an autumn with lean months ahead, at a camp lived in long enough, may dig a pit and line it with bark and grass. Plant food and dried meat keep for months in the ground (fresh meat too, in a hard frost), so families put by what they have beyond a few days' needs and open the pit when the hearth is bare. A family eats from its own pit before going hungry, and those who know how to keep food fill their pits before a cold winter. A pit cannot be carried: a band setting off takes only what its people can carry, dried food first, and the rest stays in the ground, a reason to come back.
  - **Huts in each people's style.** Building huts is the second idea, worked out by someone fetching wood at a camp lived in a long while, with the cold coming through the old shelters. A household that knows how, and hopes to stay, raises a hut a little at a time: poles fetched from the trees (or turf and reeds cut on the spot where there are none), lashed into a frame, then covered. Each people builds its own way: those who bent domes of grass raise round huts of thatch or daub, lean-to builders raise gabled longhouses of bark (where grown children add a bay to their parents' house instead of building their own), and tent makers raise tall cones of poles sheathed in bark and hides. Where it is cold the hut is sunk into the ground under turf, by a lake or in a swamp it stands on stilts, and in the heat its sides are left open under a deep thatch roof. A hut is warmer to sleep in than any shelter, keeps the hearth's food dry, and is home: it passes to a widow or the children, newlyweds move into whichever of theirs stands, an empty hut goes to the next family without one, and a hut left behind falls in slowly until only its ribs stand, teaching anyone who passes how it was done.
  - **Going home.** People remember the camp where their hut stands. A band that moved on for the summer weighs going back, to its huts and whatever is waiting in its pits, against everywhere else it knows: a seasonal round between a summer camp and the winter houses comes out of that on its own.
  - **The first fields.** A plot whose household still lives by it is not given back to the weeds after the harvest: it rests as stubble through the winter and is sown again in spring, quicker to sow on worked ground. Each crop takes something out of the soil, and a year left fallow gives it back. A household that means to stay sows more than one patch, with seed from its store or its pit. A band sows from its own harvest, and each harvest's seed is a little tamer than what was sown: over the years its grain grows plump and holds its seed until it is cut, its tubers fat, its gourds big, a seed line it takes with it when it splits.
  - **Gatherings.** Bands of one people on good terms may camp side by side, each at its own fire; strangers and bands with bad blood keep their distance. When the land is rich enough, bands moving for food come together where they find kin and friends, and their camps side by side are a gathering: visits are a short walk, young people court across fires, know-how passes by watching, and gifts go round. As the food thins, the bands set off again.
  - **Villages.** A band, or a gathering of bands, that has lived at one place through all four seasons in at least three huts is a village. It gets a name in its people's own tongue: after whoever raised the first hut ("Ama's Ford"), or after the weightiest thing that happened there (a mammoth brought down, the first weir, the first harvest, a fight, a fire that went out). The bands in a village stay their own clans, each with its fire, pot and leader. More bands can settle beside them, a village whose people have all been gone a year is given up, and a band that comes back to its ruins brings it back to life.
  - **On screen.** Huts in every style and look, from a bare frame to a ruin, under snow, with longhouses of up to three bays; storage pits with their bark lids; fields at rest; a new animation for raising a hut. Villages get a carved sign on the map, gatherings a tag and ruins a faded one: click a sign for the village card (its name and what it means, how and when it began, its bands and who leads them, whom most of its people listen to, its huts, stores, fields and weirs, and what happened there lately). The band card has a "Homes and stores" line and how long the band has camped there, the tag shows huts going up, food put by and pits opened, and the IDEAS card lists the two new ideas. The journal has a new group, "Villages and gatherings", the ribbon and the Spotlight pick up first huts, gatherings, villages and their names, and search finds villages, gatherings and ruins by name or by what the name means.

### Keys

`/` search, `J` journal, `I` ideas, `L` Spotlight, `N` next Spotlight story, `B` bonds, `,` and `.` previous and next notable moment, `H` hides the interface, `Space` pauses, `1`-`6` set the speed, `Esc` closes a panel or unpins.

## Run it

```sh
npm install
npm run dev        # play at http://localhost:5173
npm run build      # one self-contained file: dist/index.html
npm run page       # the publishable single page: dist/little-ages.html
npm test           # world, wildlife, genetics, people, sharing and feuds, discoveries, villages, and interface tests
npm run sim -- --size medium --seed 7 --years 20 --bands 12   # watch people live, headless
npm run sim -- --size medium --seed 7 --years 10 --wild       # just the wildlife
node --experimental-strip-types scripts/sweep.ts --seeds 1-16 --years 10 --save base.json   # a balance sweep: many seeds at once
node --experimental-strip-types scripts/sweep.ts --seeds 1-16 --years 10 --against base.json # ...and what a change did to them
```

The headless run prints the population, births, deaths and their causes, couples, camp moves, band splits, kills by species and food brought in, season by season, and how food was shared, how many feuds began and ended, fights, new leaders, and gifts between bands, and what has been worked out, how it was passed on, fires gone out and relit, and the weirs, harvests and dried meat, and the huts standing, going up and fallen in, the pits and the food in them, the fields sown again and resting, the best seed line, the gatherings and the villages. At the end it tells who first worked out each idea, and when, every village with its name, what the name means, how it began and whether it still stands, how long bands stayed at their camps, and how often each new idea had a chance to come to someone. `--start cradle` begins with one people in one warm homeland instead of peoples scattered across the world.

`scripts/worldpng.ts` writes PNGs of a world for every season and map mode (`node --experimental-strip-types scripts/worldpng.ts large 12 /tmp/world`).

## Layout

- `src/sim/` runs in a Web Worker and owns the world. It holds world generation (`world/`), wildlife (`wildlife.ts`), plants, pasture, small game and fish (`ecology.ts`), birds (`birds.ts`), the people (`people/`) and the tick loop (`worker.ts`).
- `src/sim/people/`: `genetics.ts` (genome, inheritance, looks), `language.ts` (sound systems and names), `culture.ts` (clothing, hair, shelters, customs), `person.ts` (what a person is), `mind.ts` (choosing goals and plans, and the words for why), `doing.ts` (carrying out each step), `walk.ts` (pathfinding by season), `band.ts` (stores and choosing camps), `society.ts` (founding, the life cycle, bands and news) and `inspect.ts` (describing anyone in words).
- `src/render/` draws it on a canvas. `renderer.ts` handles the map, chunks and clouds; `depth.ts` paints trees, animals and people in order of the row they stand on, so creatures pass behind trees north of them and in front of trees south of them; `scale.ts` sets how small creatures are drawn; `ground.ts` makes smooth close-up coasts and surf; `life.ts` draws birds, rabbits and fish; `people.ts` draws people, camps, bubbles and labels; `sprites/` holds the pixel art. `dev/sprites.html` shows every person sprite and animation (`npm run dev`, then open `/dev/sprites.html`).
- `src/sim/people/chronicle.ts` weighs events; `search.ts` finds people, bands and peoples by name and works out the relationship threads in view.
- Milestone 2 checkpoint C added `homes.ts` (storage pits, huts and who they belong to, laying out a camp around its huts, what a household would leave behind, what a band can carry) and `villages.ts` (who camps beside whom, gatherings, villages, their names and what happened at each place), with fields and seed lines in `works.ts`, `render/sprites/huts.ts` for the huts and pits, the village card in `ui/people.ts`, and `scripts/sweep.ts` for balance sweeps. `dev/huts.html` shows every hut style, look, stage and wear, and the pits, and `dev/village.html` draws whole villages (in summer, in snow and by the water) and the map's signs with the game's own renderer.
- Milestone 2 checkpoint B added `ideas.ts` (what can be known, working it out, passing it on, losing it, and the world's record of it) and `works.ts` (fish weirs and planted plots), with `ui/ideas.ts` for the IDEAS panel. `dev/works.html` shows every stage of the plots, weirs and drying racks.
- Milestone 2 checkpoint A added `sharing.ts` (hearths and the pot, asking, the sharing rounds, debts), `feuds.ts` (grudges, feuds and the sides families take, fights, go-betweens, feuds passed down), `leaders.ts` (whom each adult listens to, the band's leader and second voice, taking a proud hunter down a peg) and `friends.ts` (visits and gifts between bands).
- `src/ui/` has the world-creation screen, the heads-up display, the pinned tag, the bands list and the news feed (`people.ts`). `dock.ts` lays the panels out and slides them in and out: the toolbar toggles every panel, and H (or HIDE UI) hides the whole interface. Hiding a pinned tag leaves a name chip, and the follow carries on. Checkpoint C added `history.ts` (the log the page keeps of everything that happened), `timeline.ts` (the ribbon's arithmetic), `ribbon.ts`, `journal.ts`, `search.ts`, `spotlight.ts` with `director.ts` (what it chooses to look at) and `bonds.ts` (threads over the map).
- `src/shared/` holds randomness, noise, the palette, types and worker messages.

Nothing in the simulation is triggered by a date or a script. Everything comes from the land, the weather, and what animals and people decide to do. A test checks the people code for date triggers.
